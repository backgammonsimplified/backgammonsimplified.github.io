Sage vs GNU: Additional Details

How Stage 1 was run, checked, and interpreted

Methods, results, and evidence for the Sage versus GNU Stage 1 backgammon engine benchmark.

Look Up a Term

This page explains how Stage 1 was run, how the results were calculated, and what the evidence can and cannot show. It is the detailed companion to the main results page.

IMPORTANT REVIEWER LIMITATION

GNU reviewed the decisions made by both engines. Using the same reviewer keeps the scoring consistent, but GNU was also one of the competitors. The error rates, equity losses, roll estimates, and adjusted results on this page are GNU’s estimates. They are not neutral proof of perfect play.

How the study was set up

Four questions, four kinds of evidence

The final score, the decision review, the roll estimate, and the deeper Sage rollouts answer different questions.

Question Evidence used
Who won the matches? The completed match results
How did GNU rate the decisions? GNU’s post-match review of checker and cube decisions
How much did the rolls appear to matter? GNU’s estimate of the value added by the rolls
What happens when selected cube disagreements are examined more deeply? Sage rollouts comparing two specified actions

These results are shown separately. They are not combined into one final engine-strength score.

Match structure

The pilot ran 20 individual seven-point matches. We generated the dice files before the matches were run.

We did not take note of the exact model versions, and all of the settings in a reliable way. That’s why we built engine benchmark so for future runs anyone can rerun the analysis.

Study identifiers and analysis unit 
Study ID: sage-gnu-stage1-public-analysis-v1
Matches: 20
Match length: 7 points
Public identifiers: match_001 through match_020
Primary match-result unit: one completed match

Were the dice random?

No. The dice were deterministic.

The dice files were prepared before the matches using a pseudorandom number generator. Given the same generator, seeds, and preparation procedure, the same dice sequences can be recreated.

This is useful because a repeated study can use exactly the same dice rather than merely another random sample.

Were the dice rigged?

No.

The dice files were fixed before play. They were not changed after seeing the engines’ decisions or results, and they were not selected to help either engine.

The generator details, seeds, dice files, checksums, settings, and match files are retained so the setup can be inspected or repeated. The historical seat swaps did not create common-dice pairs, so this analysis does not assume that luck cancels between two matches.

Ply Settings

We used the default ply settings and did not used any custom configs such as move filter settings.

Checker-play ply depths

The live checker-play depths were:

GNU checker play: 3-ply
Sage checker play: 4-ply

These were the closest practical regular fixed-ply depths available through the two engine interfaces. Sage 4-ply was its strongest regular fixed-ply checker option in the interface used for this study.

GNU 4-ply was kept for the post-match review rather than live play because it took substantially longer on this setup.

Cube-decision ply depths

The live cube depths were:

GNU cube decisions: 2-ply
Sage cube decisions: 3-ply

Why these ply depths were chosen?

A ply is one layer of lookahead. As the ply depth increases, an engine examines a play, the reply, and further replies before rating the resulting position.

GNU and Sage label those layers differently. GNU starts at 0-ply, while Sage starts at 1-ply. Giving both engines the same displayed number would therefore give them different amounts of lookahead.

GNU 0-ply ~= Sage 1-ply
GNU 1-ply ~= Sage 2-ply
GNU 2-ply ~= Sage 3-ply
GNU 3-ply ~= Sage 4-ply

The goal was not to make the engines internally identical. They use different neural networks, cube models, move filters, bearoff methods, and internal procedures. The narrower goal was to avoid an obvious mismatch in ply depth while keeping the live matches practical to run and possible to repeat.

What are move filters?

Looking several plies ahead takes a great deal of time. To make deeper analysis fast enough for live play, an engine does not always carry every legal checker move through the full search.

Instead, it gives all the moves a quicker first evaluation. The moves that look most promising continue to the deeper search, while moves that appear clearly worse are removed. This shortcut is called a move filter.

GNU and Sage both use move filters, but they do not use exactly the same system. GNU’s filters control how many moves continue at each stage and how close an additional move must be to the current leader. Sage also ranks the moves at a lower ply depth, keeps a limited group near the best preliminary result, and then analyzes those moves more deeply.

A wider filter examines more moves and usually takes longer. A tighter filter is faster, but there is a greater chance that a move removed during the quick evaluation would have looked better after the full search.

For Stage 1, we matched the main ply depths, but we did not separately configure or match the move filters. The run relied on the filtering supplied by each engine’s tested version and run configuration. This helped keep the live matches practical, but it also means that matching ply depth did not make the two searches identical.

The study therefore compares the complete engine configurations that were actually used. It cannot tell us whether a difference came from the neural network, the move filter, the deeper search, or another part of the engine. A future study should record the active move-filter settings and could test wider filters to see whether the results change.

This pairing lets the player on roll have one examined turn and then lets the opponent have one examined reply before the position is evaluated.

What is examined GNU Sage
No future dice or checker plays 0-ply 1-ply
One turn for the player on roll, with no opponent reply 1-ply 2-ply
One turn for each player 2-ply 3-ply
Two turns for the player on roll and one for the opponent 3-ply 4-ply
Two turns for each player 4-ply Not available
Why not use a shallower or deeper cube pairing?

The shallower pairing, GNU 1-ply and Sage 2-ply, stops after the player on roll has moved. It does not examine the opponent’s reply. An immediate hit, escape, attack, or point may therefore look better before the engine checks whether the opponent can answer it.

The next deeper pairing, GNU 3-ply and Sage 4-ply, gives the original player two examined turns but gives the opponent only one. GNU 4-ply would give both players two turns, but Sage did not offer a matching regular 5-ply live option.

GNU 2-ply and Sage 3-ply were therefore the least uneven practical pairing for live cube decisions.

Match results

Who won?

How strong was the 13-7 result?

If the engines were equally likely to win each match, a 13-7 split in either direction, or a wider gap, would occur about 26% of the time.

The formal result is a two-sided binomial p-value of 0.263. Because this is above the study’s 0.05 threshold, the score alone was not strong enough evidence to rule out an even matchup.

This does not mean there is a 26% chance that luck caused the result. It also does not mean there is a 74% chance that GNU is stronger.

Fifteen GNU wins is the first result on that side to fall below the 0.05 threshold. Its two-sided p-value is about 0.041. The threshold is a research convention, not a point at which a result becomes certain.

Did GNU have better rolls? How much did luck matter?

Yes. GNU was luckier according to GNU’s review. That makes the official 13-7 score look more one-sided than the underlying contest may have been.

After GNU’s estimated roll advantage was removed, all 7 Sage wins still showed Sage ahead. Of GNU’s 13 wins, 8 still showed GNU ahead, 3 became nearly even, and 2 showed Sage ahead.

Twenty matches are not enough to settle the comparison, so a longer test is needed.

How was luck measured, and what changed after the adjustment?

The completed matches were reviewed at 4-ply. GNU’s luck calculation itself uses a faster 0-ply evaluation after each roll. It estimates how much better or worse the player’s position became, assuming the player then makes the move preferred by that evaluation.

For more background, read the classic article A Measure of Luck in Backgammon.

GNU estimated that it received the more valuable rolls in all 20 matches. This does not mean the dice were biased or rigged. It means that, according to GNU’s calculation, the rolls improved GNU’s chances more than Sage’s in the positions that occurred.

The adjustment also estimates whether the roll advantage was large enough to change which engine appeared ahead. All 7 Sage wins still showed Sage ahead. Among GNU’s 13 wins, 8 still showed GNU ahead, 3 became nearly even, and 2 showed Sage ahead.

This is a model-based estimate. It does not replay the matches, decide who deserved to win, or create a replacement match score.

How is the adjusted result centered?

GNU starts with a centered result and subtracts its estimate of the net dice advantage:

Observed win:  +50
Observed loss: -50
Zero:          neutral adjusted result

These are analytical values centered on zero. They are not literal final match-winning chances of 100% and 0%.

The report labels an adjusted result within plus or minus 1 match-winning-chance percentage point as nearly even. This is an editorial band, not a confidence interval or a statistical test.

How GNU reviewed the decisions

What did GNU compare?

After each match, GNU reviewed the checker plays and cube decisions made by both engines. For each decision that counted under the review rules, GNU compared the action taken with the action it preferred and estimated how much equity was lost.

Equity is the value of a position or decision after accounting for the possible match outcomes. An error rate, or ER, summarizes the estimated equity lost per eligible decision. Lower is better.

The same reviewer and review configuration were used for both engines, which keeps the calculation consistent. GNU still remains both competitor and reviewer.

What were the pooled results?

The official pooled results were:

Engine Decision type Equity loss Eligible decisions ER
Sage Checker play 0.856 1,471 0.291
GNU Checker play 0.515 1,457 0.177
Sage Cube decisions 3.880 201 9.652
GNU Cube decisions 0.010 242 0.021
Sage Overall 4.735 1,672 1.416
GNU Overall 0.523 1,699 0.154

GNU’s review found a relatively small checker-play difference and a much larger cube-decision difference. The cube decisions account for most of Sage’s reviewed equity loss.

This does not prove that every disputed Sage cube action was wrong. The separate Sage rollout study examines selected disagreements using Sage’s own deeper analysis.

How is pooled ER calculated?

The study uses True ER on the 500 scale:

\[ \text{ER} = 500 \times \frac{\text{total authoritative equity loss}} {\text{total eligible decisions}} \]

The overall calculations are:

\[ \text{Sage ER} = 500 \times \frac{4.735}{1{,}672} = 1.416 \]

\[ \text{GNU ER} = 500 \times \frac{0.523}{1{,}699} = 0.154 \]

The losses and decisions are added across all 20 matches before the rate is calculated. The 20 match ER values are not simply averaged.

An eligible checker decision is a played checker move that GNU scored under the accepted review rules. Eligible cube decisions include doubles, takes, passes, and no-double decisions that GNU considered close enough to matter. Routine no-double positions are not all included.

Because there are far fewer eligible cube decisions, one large cube error can affect cube ER much more than one checker error affects checker ER.

How much did ER vary from match to match?

Match-level ER helps show which matches were ordinary and which contained unusually large reviewed losses. It is descriptive. It is not the official combined estimate, and the 20 match values are not treated as independent observations for a significance test.

How often were cube decisions close?

The cube percentage needs an audited list of all eligible cube opportunities, including valid zero-loss decisions. It cannot be calculated from a table that contains only positive errors.

The current threshold is an absolute GNU-reviewed loss of 0.010 EMG or less. A valid zero remains in the denominator and counts as within the threshold. A missing, unparsed, or unscored opportunity is not changed to zero.

How selected cube disagreements are being checked

GNU’s review identified selected cube positions where Sage’s original action differed from GNU’s recommendation.

Those positions are examined with a deeper Sage rollout. A rollout repeatedly plays forward from the same position to compare how two actions perform across many possible continuations.

Stage 1 compares exactly two actions: Sage’s original cube action and GNU’s recommendation. A valid uncertainty range entirely on GNU’s side supports GNU. A range entirely on Sage’s side supports Sage’s original action. A range that includes zero is unresolved. A point estimate without a valid paired uncertainty range is reported separately and is not called resolved.

No conclusion is made about a third action.

PENDING AUDITED SAGE ROLLOUT RELEASE

Do not publish rollout verdicts, counts, or representative positions until the audited rollout release is available. Pending does not mean zero.

What must the audited rollout section include?

The final section must show how many positions were selected, attempted, completed, valid, resolved, unresolved, point-estimate-only, and excluded. It must report how many valid results support GNU and how many support Sage’s original action.

Each published position must include the exact match context, the two actions, the paired Sage difference in EMG, its valid uncertainty range when available, the classification, the rollout settings and trial count, and a link to the retained evidence.

The interpretation must stay within the selected positions. It must not claim that Sage’s neural evaluator is generally weaker unless a separate experiment isolates that cause.

How long did the matches and review take?

The live matches were fairly quick and consistent on this setup. The 4-ply reviews took much longer and had a much wider spread.

Stage Average Fastest Slowest How it was run
Live seven-point match Just under 15 minutes Just under 5.1 minutes Just under 30 minutes One match at a time
GNU 4-ply review Just under 68 minutes Just over 32 minutes Almost 2.5 hours Several reviews in parallel

The match times were grouped fairly closely together. In technical terms, they had lower variance, meaning there was less difference between the shorter and longer runs.

The 4-ply review times were much more spread out. Although the average was just under 68 minutes, one review could take from just over 32 minutes to almost 2.5 hours. This made the review time much less predictable.

These runtimes describe the hardware and setup used for this pilot. We ran the live matches one at a time to reduce the risk of errors. We ran several completed-match reviews in parallel, so adding all individual review times does not give the wall-clock time for the whole study.

How the results are checked and published

One audited analytical release

Every public figure and number should come from the same accepted analytical release. This prevents a page from accidentally mixing tables produced at different stages of the work.

PENDING TABLE :: AUDITED RELEASE MANIFEST

Totals are checked against their sources

For important values such as pooled equity loss and adjusted results, the release keeps the source value, the independently reconstructed value, the rounded display value, the accepted value, and the size and status of any difference.

The underlying rows are not changed merely to force agreement with a rounded number printed elsewhere.

Independent validation

The study checks that the intended engines, models, rules, cube settings, ply depths, and dice evidence were used. It also checks that the matches completed, the review settings are supported, the denominators and totals are correct, the roll orientation is consistent, and the rollout positions and actions are aligned correctly.

Figures are checked against the accepted tables, including their values, labels, units, captions, and alternative text. When practical, the person validating the release is separate from the person who produced it.

What is kept so the study can be repeated?

The public record should include the source-code commits, engine versions and models, ply depths, match rules, cube settings, dice-generation inputs, retained stream evidence, run commands, environment details, original match and review files, the audited analytical release, Sage rollout evidence, figure-to-table mappings, validation reports, retries, exclusions, manifests, and checksums.

Limitations

Limitation Why it matters
Small sample Twenty matches are not enough to establish a stable ranking. The 13-7 score did not meet the study’s 0.05 threshold.
One tested setup The findings apply to these engine versions, models, ply depths, match rules, and hardware. Other setups may give different results.
Independent dice streams The historical seat swaps were not common-dice pairs, so the analysis does not assume paired luck cancellation.
GNU was competitor and reviewer Using one reviewer keeps the arithmetic consistent, but GNU may favour decisions that resemble its own methods.
Match ER is descriptive Match-level ER helps show variation but is not the official pooled estimate or an independent sample for inference.
Roll adjustments are model-based The adjusted result depends on GNU’s evaluation. It is not a replay, corrected score, or proof of who should have won.
Presentation bands are not statistical tests The 0.010 EMG closeness threshold and the plus or minus 1 MWC nearly-even band are reporting rules, not confidence intervals.
Rollouts cover selected positions The Sage rollout results apply to the chosen cube disagreements and should not be generalized to every Sage decision.
The evaluator was not isolated This study cannot separate the neural evaluator from ply depth, move filters, cube modelling, and other engine components.

What the evidence supports

GNU won more of these 20 matches, but the 13-7 score was not strong enough to cross the study’s statistical threshold.

GNU’s own review gave GNU the lower pooled ER, with most of the reviewed difference coming from cube decisions. Both engines were very often within 0.010 EMG of GNU’s preferred checker move.

GNU estimated that it received the more valuable rolls in all 20 matches. The adjusted categories suggest that the official score may have made the contest look more one-sided than it was, but they do not create a corrected score.

The cube-closeness percentage and Sage rollout conclusions remain pending until their audited releases are available.

This is a reproducible descriptive pilot about these matches and this tested setup. It does not establish a universal or permanent ranking of Sage and GNU.
