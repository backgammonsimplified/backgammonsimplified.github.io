Why Is 25% the Basic Take Point When a Double Is Offered?

Beginner
Intermediate

Learn why a backgammon player can correctly take a double with only a 25% chance to win in a simplified money game, how taking compares with passing, and why gammons, cube ownership, future redoubles, and match score can move the real take point away from 25%.

Author

Marty Gale

Doubling Cube

Concept: Taking a double
Level: Foundation
Prerequisite: Basic doubling-cube rules

The question

Your opponent doubles the cube from 1 to 2.

You expect to lose about three games out of four.

Why could taking still be correct?

The position

Your opponent is on roll and has approximately a 75% chance to win. You have approximately a 25% chance.

Your opponent doubles. Should you take or pass?

The short answer

A take is not a prediction that you will win.

It is a comparison between two ways of losing value:

  • pass now and lose 1 point for certain;
  • take, risk losing 2 points, but keep a chance to win 2 points.

Under the simplified assumptions in this lesson, taking and passing have the same average value when your winning chance is exactly 25%.

That is why 25% is called the basic take point.

Key idea

You do not need to be the favorite to take. You only need taking to be better than surrendering the current stake.

What happens when you pass?

Passing ends the game immediately.

The cube is still at 1, so you lose 1 point.

\[ E(\text{Pass})=-1 \]

There is no uncertainty. You lose exactly 1 point every time you pass in this situation.

What happens when you take?

Taking keeps the game going with the cube at 2.

There are two simplified outcomes:

  • if you win, you gain 2 points;
  • if you lose, you lose 2 points.

At a 25% winning chance, you win one quarter of the time and lose three quarters of the time.

\[ E(\text{Take}) = 0.25(2)+0.75(-2) \]

\[ E(\text{Take}) = 0.50-1.50 = -1 \]

Taking also loses 1 point on average.

Therefore:

\[ E(\text{Take})=E(\text{Pass})=-1 \]

At exactly 25%, the two choices are equal in the simplified model.

See it across four games

Imagine reaching this decision four times.

If you pass every time, you lose 1 point in each game:

\[ 4(-1)=-4 \]

If you take every time and win one of the four games:

  • one win earns 2 points;
  • three losses cost 6 points;
  • the total is a loss of 4 points.

\[ 1(2)+3(-2)=-4 \]

Either choice loses 4 points across the four games, or 1 point per game on average.

This is the simplest way to understand the 25% take point.

Deriving the boundary

Let \(p\) be your probability of winning after taking.

Your take equity is:

\[ E(\text{Take})=2p-2(1-p) \]

Simplifying:

\[ E(\text{Take})=4p-2 \]

At the take point, taking and passing have equal value:

\[ 4p-2=-1 \]

\[ 4p=1 \]

\[ p=0.25 \]

So the simplified take point is:

\[ 25\% \]

Why this position is useful

This bearoff removes almost everything that could distract from the basic calculation:

  • there is no contact;
  • gammons and backgammons are impossible;
  • only four checkers remain for each player;
  • the winning chances are close to 75% versus 25%;
  • the cube decision can be explained with ordinary arithmetic.

The board gives us a concrete example, but the reason for the 25% boundary comes from comparing the equity of taking with the equity of passing.

Is exactly 25% a take or a pass?

In the simplified model, it is neither better nor worse. The two actions are equal.

  • above 25%, taking is better;
  • below 25%, passing is better;
  • at exactly 25%, you are indifferent.

In a real money game, the cube is usually still alive after you take. You then own the cube and may be able to redouble later. That future right can make taking slightly better than the simple calculation suggests.

For this first lesson, we deliberately set that complication aside. The next lesson explains why the practical take point can be lower than 25%.

NoteWhy GNU may show a take

If GNU calls this position a take rather than complete indifference, that does not invalidate the 25% calculation.

The calculation assumes that the cube has no future value after the take. GNU also considers the value of owning the cube and possibly redoubling later.

What 25% does not mean

Twenty-five percent is not a universal rule for every cube decision.

It does not account for:

  • gammon and backgammon wins;
  • gammon and backgammon losses;
  • future redoubles;
  • cube ownership;
  • match score;
  • position-specific volatility.

Those features change the value of taking and can move the real take point.

The 25% rule is the starting model. It gives us a clear benchmark before we add the rest of the game.

What to remember

  • Passing loses 1 point immediately.
  • Taking means playing for 2 points.
  • At a 25% winning chance, the simplified take equity is \(-1\).
  • Passing also has equity of \(-1\).
  • A correct take can still lose most of the time.
  • Twenty-five percent is a basic benchmark, not the answer to every practical cube decision.
