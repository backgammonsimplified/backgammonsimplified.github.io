Backgammon Engine Benchmark

A fair and open way to compare backgammon engines

See how backgammon engines perform in controlled matches, how their decisions are reviewed, and what the results can and cannot tell us.

The Backgammon Engine Benchmark lets engines play under controlled conditions, then publishes the results and the evidence behind them.

We ran a 20 match pilot with Sage vs GNU. After that pilot we wanted to release a robust way to fairly compare backgammon engines against each other. That’s why we built the Engine Benchmark tool.

Before you have backgammon engines fight each other, you must record the plan, settings, and how the dice were generated. You also want to record the match files, settings used for review and futher analysis . We do this for the comparison can be fair, inspected and reproduced by others.

The code and instructions to run your own tests is available in the public repository below, while the website has the stories of the battles we conducted and what it means for backgammon players like yourself.

You can configure the methods and settings as they can change from one study to another. Engines do not all offer the same tools, as some can review a complete match (GNU CLI), while others can only examine one position at a time (Sage’s current Python package).

Is the plan fixed before play?

Yes.

Before the first match, we record the engine versions, match rules, ply depths, dice procedure, review method, and the questions the study will answer. This keeps the study from being changed after an early result.

A necessary change can still be made, but it is published as a change to the original plan, and the settings should be republished/comitted BEFORE the matches are run.

Are the dice random?

No. The dice were deterministic.

The dice files were prepared before the matches using a pseudorandom number generator. Given the same generator, seeds, and preparation procedure, the same dice sequences can be recreated.

This is useful because a repeated study can use exactly the same dice rather than merely another random sample.

How are engine settings matched?

Engine settings cannot always be matched by using the same ply number.

Ply depth tells us how many decision layers an engine looks ahead. Different engines start count plying depth differently, use different types of search, or expose different analysis tools. Before a study begins, you want to documents what each setting does understand why the selected pair is the closest practical comparison.

The goal is not to make the engines internally identical. That is impossible. The goal is to avoid an obvious mismatch in ply depth while keeping the live matches practical to run.

How are the results judged?

Match wins and decision quality are different

The score tells us who won, and a binomial test can see if it’s statistically significant.

A review from a stronger engine (or engine setting) looks at the choices made during the match.

This distinction matters because an engine can make stronger decisions and still lose after receiving worse rolls. The benchmark therefore presents the score, decision review, and estimated “luck” /roll advantage as separate evidence.

The reviewer is named

We use GNU 4ply to review the decisions made by both engines. The same review method was used for both sides, which keeps the scoring consistent.

Sage doesn’t offer a match review function in it’s current Python package. We decided not to implement one as it could be error prone. Instead we just use the GNU 4 ply to evaluate matches, and compore the differences using a Sage rollout (explained in the next section)

Using GNU 4ply as a review means that it can not be considered a neutral source of perfect answers. It’s lower ply cousin also competed in the matches, and it’s likely to favour those moves at higher ply as well. The results are therefore described as GNU’s estimates, not as proof of the objectively correct decision.

We ideally would be able to BGBlitz or XG, and will be future work if we can get permission from the developers. This is always a challenge with open-source projects unfortunately. This doesn’t stop you from reviewing the match files we publish in bulk and letting us know what you find! We can publish your results too!

Some disagreements receive a deeper look

A normal match review can find positions where Sage and GNU preferred different actions. These positions can then be examined more deeply by playing out many possible continuations from the same position. This is called a rollout.

Best practice (since GNU is reviewing) is for Sage to do a rollout for the positions which GNU 4 ply said was “wrong”. We can then look at the positions and consider outselves what is the best move. Or ask another engine.

What is checked and published?

The project keeps the evidence needed to follow the comparison from the original plan to the public result. This includes the engine versions and settings, prepared dice, completed match files, review files, selected positions, deeper analysis, and validation reports.

The important checks are also recorded. We confirm that the intended engines and rules were used, the matches completed, the published totals match the source files, and the deeper analysis used the stated positions and settings. Failed checks, retries, and exclusions are not silently removed.

The public website explains the findings. The repository keeps the more detailed record for readers who want to inspect or repeat the work.

What can the benchmark tell us?

A study can show what happened between specific engine versions under specific rules and settings. It can also show how a named reviewer judged their decisions and what deeper analysis found in selected disagreements.

It cannot prove that one engine is always stronger. Backgammon results are affected by the rolls, reviewers can disagree, and different settings can produce different behaviour. Larger studies can provide stronger evidence, but every conclusion still belongs to the conditions that were tested.

What comes next?

We are going onto Stage 1 where we will test the full process with a small set of matches. The same approach can later be used for larger studies, different settings, and other engines.

Each new study will publish its plan before play and keep its results separate, so readers can see exactly which evidence supports each conclusion.
