Sage vs GNU: Stage 1

What happened across 20 seven-point matches?

Results from 20 seven-point matches between Sage and GNU, including match wins, 4ply review, luck estimates, and selected Sage rollouts.
Author

Marty Gale

Sage and GNU played 20 seven-point matches using fixed ply depths and dice files prepared before play. Guess who won?

Skeptical of our approach or results?

As you read, you may think it was unfair or unscientific, we have a dedicated page for our research method with details. This article summarizes the findings and briefly talks about the methods.

Who won the matches?

GNU won 13 of the 20 matches. Sage won 7.

Is GNU Better than Sage?

Unsure, the test was statisticaly insignificant. We need to run more matches.

What do you mean statistically insignificant? What test did you run?

If the engines were equal we would expect a 50% or 10 to 10 result. To judge the statistical significance, we asked a simple question:

If the engines had an equal chance of winning each match, given random chance, how often would we see a 13 to 7 result or better?

Using a two sided binomial test, the answer is about 26% of the time. Statisticians would say the study results show a p-value of 0.263. For the result to be significant, we would expect 5% or a p-value of 0.05. Therefore the 13-7 result is statistically insignificant. This does not mean there is a 26% chance that luck caused the result. It means the score, by itself, is not unusual enough to rule out an even matchup.

The TODO research page explains the test and shows the full distribution. You can also use this binomial-test calculator or watch this introduction to the binomial test if you are interested.

Did GNU have better rolls? How much did luck matter?

Yes. GNU was luckier according to GNU’s 4-ply review. After adjusting for the rolls, 5 of GNU’s 13 wins no longer clearly showed GNU ahead. This makes the contest look closer than the official 13-7 score.

How was luck measured? And what would the result be after a luck adjustment?

The matches were reviewed at 4-ply, but GNU’s luck calculation uses a faster 0-ply evaluation. After each roll, it estimates how much better or worse the player’s position became, assuming the player then makes the best move according to that evaluation.

For a fuller explanation, read the classic article A Measure of Luck in Backgammon.

GNU’s review estimated that GNU received the more valuable rolls in all 20 matches. This does not mean the dice were biased or rigged. It means that, according to GNU’s calculation, the rolls improved GNU’s chances more than Sage’s.

The adjustment also estimates whether that roll advantage was large enough to affect which engine appeared ahead. All 7 of Sage’s wins still showed Sage ahead. Of GNU’s 13 wins, 8 still showed GNU ahead, 3 became nearly even, and 2 showed Sage ahead.

This adjustment is an estimate, not a replacement match score.

IMPORTANT LIMITATION

We used GNU 4 ply to review the matches and then a Sage rollout to resolve the arguments. GNU 4ply will of course favour GNU’s lower ply decisions.

Why did we choose GNU to review itself?

GNU has an open source interface with a review tool for match files. We want to open-source the results, so anyone, including yourself to reproduce the result. We can review with other engines in the future, if we can get the developer approval.

How is that fair? Isn’t it biased? What about rollouts?

We use GNU 4ply to review the decisions made by both engines. The same review method was used for both sides, which keeps the scoring consistent.

Sage Python Package doesn’t offer a match review function, only evaluate positions. We decided not to implement a match review as it could be error prone. Instead we just replied on GNU 4 ply to evaluate matches, and compare the differences using a Sage rollout.

We ideally would be able to BGBlitz or XG, and will be future work if we can get permission from the developers. This is always a challenge with open-source projects unfortunately. This doesn’t stop you from reviewing the match files we publish in bulk and letting us know what you find! We can publish your results too!

How did the review rate the decisions?

GNU had a lower ER. The difference in checker play was smaller, but Sage’s ER is much higher for Cube decisions.

Engine Overall ER
Sage 1.416
GNU 0.154
How do you calculate ER?

After the matches, GNU compared each eligible checker and cube decision with the “best move”. It then estimated how much equity was lost by making the incorrect decision. Equity is the engine’s estimate of how valuable a position or decision is, based on the possible outcomes.

The way to calculate ER (Error Rate) is to take the sum of all equity loss (from the “mistakes”), divides it by the number of all non-forced decisions, and multiplies by 500. This produces the pooled error rate, or ER. Lower is better. We do not average the ER of the 20 matches. This is the same way BMAB calculates equity.

The Overall ER is calculated from the combined equity losses and all non-forced decisions. It is not the average of the 20 separate match ER values. A forced decision is for example when you roll while on the bar and not being able to come in, or when you are bearing in, there is only 1 available move. GNU review automatically calculates this, we do not.

In our case:

\[ \text{Sage ER} = 500 \times \frac{4.735}{1{,}672} = 1.416 \]

\[ \text{GNU ER} = 500 \times \frac{0.523}{1{,}699} = 0.154 \]

Where did the reviewed difference come from?

Most of the gap came from cube decisions.

Decision type Sage ER GNU ER
Checker play 0.291 0.177
Cube decisions 9.652 0.021

GNU’s review found a relatively small difference in checker play and a much larger difference in cube decisions. Sage’s reviewed cube losses therefore account for most of its overall ER.

This does not prove that every disputed Sage cube decision was wrong. GNU made the judgment, and the separate Sage rollout study examines selected disagreements more deeply.

How close were the checker plays?

The ER values tell us the total reviewed loss. It also helps to ask how often each played move was very close to GNU’s preferred move.

GNU reports the gap on a normalized equity scale called EMG. EMG means equivilent money game, which is how we can compare the equity lost in different match formats. This is called “normalization”. To learn more, you can check out a class article on Normalizing Error.

Even 4 ply analysis doesn’t garuntee the correct move, so we considered if a checker decision was within 0.010 EMG equity of GNU’s preferred play, it to be the “correct” play.

Engine Decisions within 0.010 EMG Share
Sage 1,448 of 1,471 98.4%
GNU 1,444 of 1,457 99.1%

Both engines were close to 4 ply’s preferred move on the great majority of eligible checker decisions. This is not objective accuracy. It measures closeness to 4ply’s judgment.

A checker decision with zero reviewed loss stays in the total and counts as close. Zero-loss decisions may be left out of a separate plot of non-zero mistakes so the visible errors are easier to compare, but they are not removed from these percentages.

What happens when Sage and GNU disagree on the cube?

GNU’s review found selected cube decisions where Sage’s original action differed from GNU’s recommendation.

Those positions are being examined with a deeper Sage rollout. In a rollout, Sage repeatedly plays forward from the same position to compare how the two actions perform over many continuations. Stage 1 compares only Sage’s original action with GNU’s recommendation. A rollout may support GNU, support Sage’s original action, or remain too close to call.

Why did the engines use different ply depths?

A ply is one layer of lookahead. The engine examines a play, then a reply, then another reply as the ply depth increases.

GNU and Sage label those layers differently. GNU starts at 0-ply, while Sage starts at 1-ply. Giving both engines the same displayed number would therefore give them different amounts of lookahead.

Stage 1 used:

Checker play
GNU: 3-ply
Sage: 4-ply

Cube decisions
GNU: 2-ply
Sage: 3-ply

For checker play, these were the closest practical fixed-ply depths available in both engines.

For cube decisions, GNU 2-ply and Sage 3-ply both look ahead by both the player on roll and the reply by the opponent. If you use an odd number ply such as 3 ply for GNU, it only considers the player move and not the reply. To simplify, it makes a cube decisions where “riskier” moves are considered better because it isn’t looking for the reply of the opponent.

If that’s confusing, we explain it in detail in our research article

Matching the amount of lookahead makes the comparison fairer, but it does not make the engines identical. They still use different neural networks, cube models, move filters, and internal methods.

How long did it take to run?

How long did the matches and analysis take?

The live matches were fairly quick and consistent on our setup.

Figure 1

Figure 2

The match times were grouped fairly closely together. In technical terms, they had relatively low variance, meaning there was less difference between the shorter and longer matches.

The 4-ply review times were much more spread out. Although the average was just under 68 minutes, an individual review could take anywhere from just over 32 minutes to almost 2.5 hours. This made the analysis time much less predictable than the match time.

These results are specific our specific hardware and setup. For this pilot, we ran the live matches one at a time to reduce the risk of errors. We ran the 4-ply reviews in parallel, which allowed several completed matches to be analyzed at the same time.

What does this study tell us?

In these 20 matches, GNU won more often and received the lower ER under GNU’s own post-match review. Most of the reviewed ER gap came from cube decisions. The checker plays of both engines were very often close to GNU’s preferred move.

GNU’s roll estimates suggest that the official 13-7 score may make the contest look more one-sided than it was. The adjusted result is still an estimate, not a corrected score. The cube-closeness percentage and Sage rollout conclusions also remain pending until their audited releases are ready.

This is evidence about these 20 matches, these engine versions, and these ply depths. It does not establish a permanent ranking between Sage and GNU.
